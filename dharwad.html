DHARWAD CHRONICLES

History of the Scholarly Hub

Dharwad functioned historically as a major gateway between the Malnad hills and northern Deccan plains. Ruled across centuries by the Chalukyas, the Adil Shahis, and later emerging as a crucial learning seat under British administrative eras, it evolved a legendary legacy in Hindustani classical music, literature, and historic stone design variants.

01 / 10

Chandramouleshwara Temple

A beautiful 900-year-old Western Chalukyan architectural monument located in Unkal, featuring a unique four-sided door design framework and highly polished stone pillars.

Dating back to the late 11th or early 12th century during the reign of the Western Chalukyas, this structural masterpiece is dedicated to Lord Shiva. Built using fine-grained gray-green soapstone (chloritic schist), it represents the pinnacle of Chalukyan experimentation with multi-directional temple layouts. Unlike standard square or rectangular shrines, its floor plan features dynamic projections and recesses that create stunning lighting variations throughout the day.

The main sanctuary houses a Chaturmukha Linga (a four-faced Shiva manifestation), matching the architectural motif of four entry doors facing north, south, east, and west. The circular stone pillars inside the main assembly hall were turned on primitive mechanical lathes, resulting in a glass-like finish that still reflects light effortlessly after nine centuries of exposure to elements.

02 / 10

Siddharoodha Math Complex

A vast spiritual and historical center housing monolithic assembly spaces, built to honor the life and egalitarian philosophical activities of Saint Siddharoodha Swamy.

Sri Siddharoodha Swamy (1836–1929) was an extraordinary ascetic who chose Hubli-Dharwad as his primary home to spread Advaita Vedanta philosophy. He spent his life actively speaking out against caste-based discrimination, teaching that spiritual realization should be completely open to everyone regardless of background or identity. The institution grew around his humble mud cottage, eventually transforming into a sprawling, vibrant stone monastic complex.

In 1924, Mahatma Gandhi visited this ashram to seek the Swamy’s counsel during the Indian independence movement, cementing its place within modern history. Today, the central Samadhi temple features a prominent multi-tiered tower decorated with classic stucco work. The complex continues to run massive community kitchens (Dasoha), serving free meals to thousands of daily travelers and seekers.

03 / 10

Karnatak College Building

A majestic early 20th-century colonial brick structure featuring an elegant central dome, originally designed to serve as a regional railway administrative headquarters.

Constructed in the late 1880s by the Southern Mahratta Railway Company, this sprawling building was meant to anchor British rail transport across the Deccan plains. However, the administrative plans shifted, and the complex was handed over to the education department. In 1917, it opened its doors as Karnatak College, fulfilling a long-standing community demand for a top-tier educational institution in northern Karnataka.

The architecture stands out as a pristine example of Indo-Saracenic and classic British institutional styles, featuring long open verandas, thick stone arches, and deep red brickwork engineered to keep the interior cool during harsh summer months. Over the past century, this iconic campus has nurtured generations of premier writers, artists, politicians, and scientists, transforming Dharwad into a famous educational hub.

04 / 10

Amruteshwara Temple (Annigeri)

Constructed entirely out of deep black soapstone by the Western Chalukyas, this prototype temple features 76 highly detailed pillars and historic inscriptions.

Built around 1050 AD during the golden age of King Someshvara I, the Amruteshwara Temple at Annigeri served as the direct design prototype for the grander architectural marvels later built at Gadag, Lakkundi, and Itagi. It marks the precise historical transition point from earlier Badami Chalukyan stone layouts to more complex, decorative styles.

The structure is supported by 76 large pillars, each adorned with miniature carvings of dynamic deities, mythical animals, and stories from ancient Epics. The site also holds over 20 foundational stone inscriptions written in old Kannada script. These records offer a fascinating look into the medieval political shifts, trade guilds, and royal donations that shaped the economic life of the Dharwad district.

05 / 10

Dharwad Fort Gate Site

The historical stone gateway remains of a fortified complex constructed under late Adil Shahi supervision and subsequently reinforced by Maratha governors.

The Dharwad Fort was originally constructed in 1403 AD by a local commander named Dhar Rao, under the oversight of the Vijayanagara Empire. Following the empire's decline, the fort became a highly contested strategic asset. It was expanded by the Sultanate of Bijapur (Adil Shahis), captured by the Maratha empire under Shivaji Maharaj in 1678, modified by Haider Ali and Tipu Sultan, and eventually taken over by the British East India Company in 1791.

This historic outpost witnessed the famous rebellion led by Baba Saheb of Nargund and Kittur Rani Channamma’s forces against British colonial expansion. While much of the inner residential fort walls were dismantled during later town planning, the remaining massive stone gateway bastions and heavy iron arches still stand as a proud architectural reminder of Dharwad's military importance.

06 / 10

Shambulinga Temple (Kundgol)

An outstanding 11th-century Chalukyan stone structure displaying exceptionally fine mythological reliefs along its massive interior ceiling slabs.

Located in the historic town of Kundgol, this temple was built in the late 11th century using dark chloritic schist stone. It is dedicated to Shiva and stands out as a pristine example of Western Chalukyan court art, which directly influenced the later, hyper-detailed Hoysala style.

The central assembly hall (Sabha Mandapa) features beautiful lathe-turned pillars that hold up massive, intricately carved ceiling panels. These panels depict Shiva dancing the Tandava, surrounded by the solar system, musical ensembles, and guardians of the eight cardinal directions (Ashta Dikpalakas). Kundgol holds another unique historical legacy: it served as the birthplace of the Kirana Gharana, where music legends like Sawai Gandharva and Pandit Bhimsen Joshi trained.

07 / 10

All Saints Church

An elegant colonial-era gabled-roof church built by British administrators in 1888, crafted using local teakwood timber beams and laterite masonry blocks.

Consecrated in 1888 to serve the growing spiritual needs of British military officers, railway staff, and administrative workers stationed in the Dharwad residency area, this quiet church reflects the simple beauty of English village church designs.

The structural layout features a classic expansive nave supported by thick, seasoned teakwood beams sourced from the nearby forests of Malnad. The walls are built from local laterite blocks covered in lime plaster. The original altar section still keeps its beautiful stained-glass windows imported from England, which cast vibrant colorful patterns across the timber pews during morning services.

08 / 10

Asar Mahal (Dharwad)

A compact, historic medieval repository hall built in the late 17th century by Adil Shahi officers to preserve sacred civic artifacts and host local courts.

Built under the direction of Bijapur Sultanate governors before the region transitioned to Maratha control, the Asar Mahal was designed as a sacred civic space. It was used to house holy relics, display historical items, and host the high courts of justice presided over by local Islamic judges (Qazis).

The architecture features a unique blending of Persian style elements with Deccan stone engineering. The upper level is supported by tall wooden pillars, creating an open veranda that overlooks a manicured courtyard. Inside, fragments of original floral wall frescoes can still be seen, showing the artistic tastes of the late medieval elite before British urban developments changed the landscape.

Venkateshwara Temple (Sathoda)

An ancient village temple built from stone and wood, preserving early style wooden carvings and unique regional icons.

Tucked away in the quiet village of Sathoda within the Dharwad district, this humble temple represents the rural style of community worship centers that existed alongside grand royal projects. Built in the late medieval period, it served as a focal point for the religious and cultural life of the surrounding agricultural villages.

The temple stands out for its well-preserved timber framing. The roof and main hall supports are carved from durable local jackwood, featuring detailed reliefs of guardian motifs, floral patterns, and figures of deities. The primary stone deity inside reflects an early iconographical style, making this rural sanctuary an important site for historians studying the evolutionary development of temple art outside of mainstream royal courts.

10 / 10

Navalgund Weaving Clusters

The historic home of geographically-tagged (GI) Jamkhalam floor rugs, meticulously hand-woven here using traditional pit loom systems.

The art of weaving Navalgund Jamkhalam rugs dates back to the 16th century, brought to the region by a group of master weavers from the pathan community who migrated during the rule of the Ali Adil Shah Sultanate. They set up specialized pit looms in Navalgund town, creating a world-renowned weaving center.

Woven entirely by hand on traditional pit looms, these famous rugs feature bold geometric patterns, stylized bird motifs (like peacocks), and rich contrasting colors. Because of their exceptional durability and unique design style, Navalgund Jamkhalam carpets were granted India's prestigious Geographical Indication (GI) status. Visiting the active weaving clusters offers a rare, hands-on look at a living craft tradition that has been passed down directly through families for generations.
