KODAGU CHRONICLES

History of the Land of Warriors

Kodagu (Coorg) was independently ruled by the Haleri Dynasty from 1600 until British annexation in 1834. The distinct tribal warrior culture of the Kodavas, coupled with their rugged terrain, kept it semi-isolated. This independence left an array of distinct mountain fortresses, weaponry vaults, and architectural royal tombs amidst extensive misty coffee estates.

01 / 10

Madikeri Fort

Originally constructed out of mud by Muddu Raja in the late 17th century, it was later rebuilt in stone by Tipu Sultan and features a multi-tiered palace layout inside.

Madikeri Fort stands as an architectural witness to shifting empires. When the Haleri king Muddu Raja established Madikeri as his capital in 1681, he framed the stronghold with mud battlements. Its strategic vantage point overlooking the misty Western Ghats valleys made it a key conquest target during regional power struggles.

In the late 18th century, Tipu Sultan seized the fortress and rebuilt it completely with thick granite blocks, renaming it Jaffarabad. The site contains an unusual mix of landmarks: two life-sized mortar elephants stand at the entrance gates, while the inner grounds feature a two-story British-remodeled palace, an old structural church now acting as a local historical archive, and a historic secretory complex.

02 / 10

Raja's Tomb (Gaddige)

Beautiful royal monuments built in an Indo-Saracenic style with prominent central domes, containing the mortal remains of the prominent Haleri kings.

The Gaddige monument complex displays a unique blend of cultures, built between 1809 and 1821. Standing proudly on a hill on the outskirts of Madikeri, these structures show a distinct Islamic architectural influence, which is highly unusual for royal resting places built by Hindu dynasties in Southern India.

The three elegant tombs feature large central domes flanked by minaret-style corner pillars, yet the windows are carved with delicate brass grills and wall carvings of Nandi bulls and Hindu figures. The main central structure holds the remains of King Doddavira Rajendra and his queen Mahajammanni, while the neighboring structures house the tombs of Lingarajendra II and royal priest Rudrappa, preserving the history of the Haleri lineage.

03 / 10

Omkareshwara Temple

Built in 1820 by King Lingarajendra II, it features an extraordinary architectural blend of Islamic dome structures and classic Gothic lines.

The creation of the Omkareshwara Temple is deeply tied to historical folklore. Local chronicles state that King Lingarajendra II constructed this shrine to ease his troubled conscience after unjustifiably taking the life of a pious local Brahmin named Subbaraya, who had opposed the king's political choices.

To honor the spirit of the Brahmin, the king brought a sacred Shiva Linga from Kashi and built a temple that deliberately avoided traditional South Indian Dravidian elements. The building looks much like an old fort or palace, featuring a large central pool filled with freshwater fish and a unique entry layout that remains a symbol of Coorg's architecture.

04 / 10

Namdroling Monastery (Golden Temple)

A majestic Tibetan settlement featuring the 'Golden Temple', housing spectacular 12-meter tall gold-plated statues of Buddha and complex wall frescoes.

Namdroling Monastery stands as a monumental home for Tibetan heritage in exile. It was founded in 1963 by His Holiness Pema Norbu Rinpoche, starting as a simple bamboo structure built on land provided by the Indian government to shelter Tibetan refugees fleeing regional conflicts.

Over the decades, it grew into one of the largest Buddhist centers in the world, home to thousands of monks and nuns. The main prayer hall, known as the Golden Temple, features magnificent architecture adorned with detailed murals that tell stories of ancient Buddhist traditions. Inside, three massive gold-plated statues—Buddha Shakyamuni, Guru Padmasambhava, and Buddha Amitayus—gaze down over the grand hall.

05 / 10

Nalknad Palace

Built in 1792 by King Dodda Vira Rajendra, this two-storied wooden palace features beautiful wall murals, dark wood pillars, and secret underground vaults.

Nalknad Palace served as a secure royal refuge rather than a display of royal wealth. King Dodda Vira Rajendra built this palace at the base of Coorg's highest mountain peak, Tadiandamol, to protect his family during ongoing conflicts with Tipu Sultan's forces.

The palace is built with heavy dark wood pillars, sloping tiled roofs, and thick walls that offered excellent protection. The inner rooms feature vibrant ceiling paintings and murals showing historic court gatherings. Notably, this quiet mountain palace is also where Chikkavira Rajendra, the very last king of Coorg, took refuge before surrendering to British forces in 1834.

06 / 10

Talakaveri Temple Shrine

The sacred geographic point of origin of the Kaveri River, featuring historic bathing tanks and hillside shrines built along the slopes of Brahmagiri Hill.

Perched high on the slopes of Brahmagiri Hill at an altitude of 1,276 meters, Talakaveri holds a deep place in regional history and spiritual tradition. According to ancient legends, the river goddess Kaveri flowed from this spot after being released from the water vessel of Sage Agastya, cascading down to nourish the plains of South India.

The site features a small, sacred spring known as the Kundike. Devotees gather here during the annual Tula Sankramana festival in October to witness a natural upwelling of water, which is revered as a manifestation of the river goddess. Visitors can also climb a steep flight of steps up Brahmagiri Hill, offering sweeping views of the mist-covered mountain landscape.

07 / 10

Bhagamandala Triveni Sangam

A sacred confluence of three rivers featuring the historic Sri Bhagandeshwara temple complex, built in a traditional multi-tiered sloping roof architecture style.

Bhagamandala marks the sacred confluence of three rivers: the Kaveri, the Kanika, and the underground mythical Sujyothi River. This geographic landmark has been a vital center for spiritual gatherings and pilgrimage rites since early medieval times.

Facing the river ghats is the prominent Sri Bhagandeshwara temple complex, which houses shrines dedicated to Shiva, Vishnu, Subramanya, and Ganesha. The buildings feature beautiful multi-tiered tiled roofs and carved wood paneling that reflect the unique architecture of regions facing heavy monsoon rainfall. The site was briefly occupied and altered by Tipu Sultan, who renamed it Afzalabad, before being restored to its original form by the Haleri kings.

08 / 10

Padi Igguthappa Temple

Constructed in 1810 by King Lingarajendra II, this stands as the most vital ancestral shrine of the Kodava people, built in traditional regional styles.

The Padi Igguthappa Temple holds a central place in the cultural life of the Kodava community. Dedicated to Lord Igguthappa, a powerful ancient deity of rain, agriculture, and harvest, the shrine was rebuilt in 1810 by King Lingarajendra II with a design that shares many elements with the traditional architecture of neighboring Kerala.

The temple sits atop a beautiful, verdant hill in the village of Kakkabe. It features a simple exterior layout with a main sanctuary covered in polished brass panels, surrounded by heavy wooden beams and a central open-air courtyard. The temple is the focal point for the annual Huttari harvest festival, where initial harvest offerings are ritually presented to the deity before celebrations begin across Coorg.

09 / 10

St. Mark's Church Museum

An elegant, colonial British church building dating back to 1855, now transformed into a museum holding historic weapon collections and royal edicts.

St. Mark's Church was built in 1855 by the East India Company to serve the growing population of British officers, planters, and administrators who settled in Coorg's temperate climate after the annexation of the region. The building features an elegant neo-Gothic design with high arched windows and beautiful stained glass panels.

After India gained independence, the building was taken over by the state government and carefully converted into a historical museum managed by the Department of Archaeology. Today, the old church hall displays an impressive collection of heritage artifacts, including ancient stone sculptures, weapons used by Kodava warriors, British-era cannons, and old royal documents from the Haleri kings.

10 / 10

Angling Heritage Site (Valnur)

An early colonial fishing campsite along the banks of the Kaveri River, preserving old fishing lodges and conservation histories from the British era.

Valnur holds a unique place in the history of wildlife conservation and sport fishing in Southern India. During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, British coffee planters established this scenic spot along the Kaveri River as a premier site for angling, particularly for catching the famous Golden Mahseer.

To protect the river ecosystem and prevent overfishing, early sport organizations introduced strict catch-and-release rules and mandatory fishing permits, which helped safeguard the local fish populations. Today, Valnur remains a peaceful eco-tourism site surrounded by dense bamboo groves and forest trails, preserving the heritage of early environmental conservation efforts in Coorg.
